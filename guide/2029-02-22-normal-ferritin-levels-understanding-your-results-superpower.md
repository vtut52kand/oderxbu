What ferritin actually measures

Think of ferritin as your body's iron warehouse manager. This protein complex stores iron safely until your body needs it for making red blood cells, supporting immune function, or powering cellular energy production.

Unlike serum iron, which fluctuates throughout the day based on recent meals and timing, ferritin levels remain relatively stable. This makes it a reliable indicator of your total iron stores. When your body runs low on iron, ferritin levels drop first, often before you develop iron deficiency anemia.

Most people look up ferritin after receiving blood test results that seem confusing or concerning. You might have normal hemoglobin but low ferritin, indicating your iron stores are depleted even though you're not anemic yet. Or perhaps your ferritin came back unexpectedly high, prompting questions about what that means for your health.

Normal versus optimal ferritin levels

Laboratory reference ranges for ferritin cast a wide net: roughly 12-150 ng/mL for women and 12-300 ng/mL for men. These population-based ranges mean "normal" encompasses people with very different health outcomes.

Some practitioners target a narrower optimal ferritin window, though evidence for specific "optimal" cutoffs is limited. Athletes and people with heavy menstrual periods often function better at the higher end of the laboratory reference range.

Your individual trend matters more than hitting a specific number. If your ferritin consistently runs around 40 ng/mL and you feel energetic, that's likely optimal for you. But if it drops from 80 to 25 ng/mL over several months, that downward trend deserves attention regardless of staying within the "normal" range.

Age and gender considerations

Ferritin levels naturally vary by life stage. Children typically have lower levels than adults, while postmenopausal women see ferritin rise as they stop losing iron through monthly periods. Men generally maintain higher ferritin levels throughout adulthood since they don't experience regular iron loss.

What high ferritin levels can mean

Ferritin above 150 ng/mL in women or 300 ng/mL in men raises questions about why your body is storing excess iron. High ferritin often reflects inflammation rather than true iron overload, since ferritin acts as an acute-phase reactant that rises during illness, infection, or chronic inflammatory conditions.

Hereditary hemochromatosis causes iron accumulation in organs like the liver, heart, and pancreas. Men with hemochromatosis typically show ferritin levels above 300 ng/mL, while women often don't develop elevated levels until after menopause.

Other conditions associated with high ferritin include liver disease, metabolic syndrome, chronic kidney disease, and certain cancers. Alcohol consumption can also raise ferritin levels. Recent blood transfusions or iron supplementation will naturally elevate ferritin as well.

The key is distinguishing inflammation versus iron overload when ferritin is elevated. Your care team will typically check additional markers like transferrin saturation and consider genetic testing if iron overload seems likely.

What low ferritin levels can mean

Low ferritin is associated with depleted iron stores, even if your hemoglobin remains normal. This represents the earliest stage of iron deficiency, occurring before you develop iron deficiency anemia. Many people experience fatigue, weakness, and difficulty concentrating at this stage.

Heavy menstrual bleeding is the most common cause of low ferritin in premenopausal women, and prolonged heavy periods can outpace your body's ability to replenish iron stores. Pregnancy and breastfeeding also increase iron demands significantly.

Gastrointestinal bleeding depletes iron stores gradually. This might occur from ulcers, inflammatory bowel disease, or regular use of anti-inflammatory medications. Poor iron absorption due to celiac disease, Crohn's disease, or gastric bypass surgery can also lead to low ferritin despite adequate dietary intake.

Frequent blood donors are at risk of iron deficiency if they donate regularly without sufficient recovery time. Each whole-blood donation removes roughly 200-250 mg of iron, which takes months to replace through dietary absorption.

How ferritin is tested

Ferritin testing requires a simple blood draw from a vein in your arm, similar to other routine blood work. No special preparation is needed - you don't need to fast or avoid any foods or medications beforehand. The blood sample can be collected at any time of day since ferritin levels don't fluctuate significantly with meals or circadian rhythms.

Results typically return within 24-48 hours from most laboratories. The test measures ferritin concentration in nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL), though some labs report in micrograms per liter (μg/L). The numbers are equivalent - 50 ng/mL equals 50 μg/L.

How often should you retest ferritin? If your levels are normal and stable, annual testing alongside routine blood work makes sense. If you're treating iron deficiency with supplements, recheck every 8-12 weeks to monitor response. Those with iron overload conditions may need more frequent monitoring every 3-6 months.

Recent illness, infection, or inflammation can temporarily elevate ferritin. If your ferritin comes back unexpectedly high and you've been sick recently, consider retesting in 4-6 weeks once you've fully recovered.

What can change ferritin

Oral iron supplementation is the most direct way to raise low ferritin levels. Iron supplements typically increase ferritin gradually over 8-12 weeks of consistent use. Taking iron with vitamin C enhances absorption, while calcium, coffee, and tea can interfere with uptake.

Connecting ferritin to related biomarkers

Ferritin tells only part of your iron story. Serum iron, TIBC, and transferrin saturation complete the iron panel. Serum iron measures circulating iron, TIBC indicates how much iron your blood can carry, and transferrin saturation shows what percentage of that capacity is being used.

A complete iron panel reveals patterns that single markers miss. Low ferritin confirms iron deficiency when TIBC is high and transferrin saturation low. High ferritin with high transferrin saturation suggests iron overload. High ferritin with normal or low transferrin saturation points toward inflammation rather than excess iron.

Hemoglobin and hematocrit show whether iron deficiency has progressed to anemia. You can have low ferritin (depleted iron stores) with normal hemoglobin, but once ferritin drops very low, hemoglobin will eventually fall too.

Inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein help interpret elevated ferritin. High ferritin with elevated CRP suggests inflammation is driving the ferritin increase, while high ferritin with normal CRP raises more concern for true iron overload.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a normal ferritin level for a woman?

Normal ferritin levels for women typically range from 12-150 ng/mL. Premenopausal women tend to have lower ferritin due to monthly iron losses, while postmenopausal women usually see higher levels.

Can ferritin levels be too high?

Yes, ferritin above 150 ng/mL in women or 300 ng/mL in men is considered elevated. High ferritin can reflect inflammation, liver disease, or iron overload conditions like hemochromatosis. Additional testing helps determine the underlying cause.

How quickly can ferritin levels change?

Ferritin levels change gradually over weeks to months. Iron supplementation raises ferritin over 8-12 weeks, while iron loss from heavy bleeding drops levels over several months. Acute inflammation can temporarily spike ferritin within days.

Do I need to fast for a ferritin test?

No fasting is required for ferritin testing. Unlike serum iron, which fluctuates with meals, ferritin levels remain relatively stable throughout the day. You can eat normally and take medications as usual before your ferritin blood draw.

What's the difference between ferritin and iron levels?

Ferritin measures stored iron, while serum iron measures the iron currently circulating in your bloodstream. Ferritin provides a more stable indicator of your total iron reserves, while serum iron can fluctuate based on recent meals and time of day.

What causes ferritin levels to rise suddenly?

Ferritin is an acute-phase reactant, meaning it rises quickly during inflammation, infection, or illness. Conditions like rheumatoid arthritis, liver disease, metabolic syndrome, and even recent illness can spike ferritin within days. If your ferritin is unexpectedly high after being sick, consider retesting once you've recovered.

How do I raise low ferritin naturally?

Dietary changes can gradually raise low ferritin over time. Red meat, poultry, and fish provide heme iron that is easily absorbed, while pairing plant-based iron sources with vitamin C can boost non-heme iron uptake. Avoiding coffee or tea with meals also helps, as they can reduce iron absorption by 40% or more.
