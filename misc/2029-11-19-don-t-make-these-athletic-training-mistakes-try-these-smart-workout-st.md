# Don't Make These Athletic Training Mistakes. Try These Smart Workout Strategies Instead.

[These Smart](https://tissue-renewal.china-akan.com/)

THERE'S A REASON weightlifters often talk about strength training in terms of "sculpting" and "chiseling:" Their goal is a statuesque physique. Washboard abs, lobster claw triceps, teardrop quads, a perfect V-shaped torso—all of the physical qualities chased by modern bodybuilders are the same as those immortalized in the anatomical sculptures of ancient Greece and Rome.

[These Athletic Training](https://mendstride.snaketongueofficial.com/)

But if you’re not a dedicated physique athlete, there’s an inherent downside in pursuing that kind of single-minded training: “In real life, you don’t want to move like a statue,” says MH fitness director Ebenezer Samuel, C.S.C.S.

Most guys don't realize this until it's too late. But even if you think you only want mountains of muscle, there's a good chance you also want a body that performs just as well in the real world as it does in the gym. You want to be able to run quickly and nimbly, play sports with power and agility, roll around effortlessly with your kids, and generally walk through life feeling lithe and fit. “To achieve that, you have to spend at least a little bit of time training like an athlete,” says Samuel.

Does that mean performing endless drills like Amon-Ra St. Brown or never-ending intervals like Usain Bolt? Certainly not. All you have to do to kick your athleticism up a level is avoid these four training mistakes, and dive into our Ultimate Athlete training program for even more ways to move better while still leveling up your muscle and strength.

Don’t Hyperfocus on Hypertrophy

WE GET IT—your primary objective in the gym is to build muscle. It’s the reason you started lifting in the first place, and there isn’t a man alive who wouldn’t love to look better shirtless. But if you focus too intently on hypertrophy, the bulk of your training will be comprised of relatively “slow” lifts—especially if you do a lot of eccentric training.

The result is that you never get to be explosive, a key component of athleticism. “If you’re never training that explosiveness, you’re not going to make it better, and it might start to diminish,” says Samuel.

DO THIS INSTEAD:

Perform at Least One Explosive Exercise Per Workout

“If you’re a veteran lifter, lead off your workout with an explosive move such as a push press, a clean, a snatch, or even a box jump,” says Samuel. These are high skill moves, but they all force your large, type II muscle fibers to fulfill one of their primary functions: twitching fast.

Fortunately, since these are the same fibers that are targeted during heavier, slower lifting, you’ll also boost hypertrophy. It’s a win-win situation no matter how you look at it.

If you’re a beginner, save the explosive exercise for the end of your workout to avoid “pre-exhausting” your muscles before your heavier, traditional lifts. The kettlebell swing and kettlebell clean are good options here, says Samuel, who advises performing a few sets of 40 seconds on, 40 seconds off. You’ll have to use slightly lighter loads than you would if you did these exercises at the beginning of your workout, but you’ll still build explosiveness and athleticism quickly.

Don’t Do Tons of Reps

“ONE OF THE greatest misconceptions about building athleticism is that you get there by doing tons and tons of reps,” says Samuel. If you’re really trying to boost your athletic skillset—running faster, jumping higher, moving more explosively—you need to focus on movement quality, not quantity, when you’re performing explosive movements like those mentioned above.

“Quality movement will lead to better mechanics, and better mechanics will lead to athletic gains,” says Samuel.

Make Every Rep Count

In practice, that means doing fewer, higher-quality reps. If you’re performing box jumps to improve your vertical leap, for example, do just five perfectly executed reps per set instead of 15 to 20 sloppy ones. That level of quality—really focusing on each phase of each jump (take off, landing, getting down, resetting)—will build the neurological connections you need to be successful, says Samuel.

Don’t Skip Your Warmup

MAYBE YOU SKIP warming up because you’re pressed for time. Maybe you skip it because you find it boring. Either way, you’re short-changing your gains by not priming your muscles for peak performance. Indeed, you’re sending them into the training session cold and stiff.

“If you don’t warm up, your body isn’t ready to be explosive and powerful,” says Samuel. “And that stiffness will hold you back from getting the most out of your workout.”

Ramp-Up Sets

If you don’t have time for a proper warm-up, perform two ramp-up sets before your first “real” set of an explosive exercise. In practice, that means performing two sets of the given exercise at half intensity. If your main exercise is 50-meter sprints, you’re going to jog 50 meters twice beforehand, for example.

“Now you’ve got a little bit of work in, you’ve got yourself a little warmed up, and you’ve got yourself in a mindset to attack the exercise,” says Samuel. In short, you’re good to go.

Don’t Rely on Cardio and Endurance Work

THE BIGGEST MISTAKE people make when trying to train athletically is thinking that steady-state cardio doubles as athletic training. News flash: It doesn’t—unless you’re an endurance athlete. But assuming you’re not a long-distance runner (or rower, or cyclist, or open-water swimmer), steady-state cardio won’t improve your overall athleticism.

“It’s easy to go out on a five-mile run or get on the rower or skier for 20 minutes and think that because you’re breathing hard, you have somehow become more athletic,” says Samuel. But during those cardio sessions, you really aren’t focusing on the quality of or force behind each stride or stroke—two of the keys to improving athleticism, he adds.

Sprint Intervals

Interval training is one of the only true “double-duty” exercises. Not only will it give you a serious cardiovascular pump, but it will also help you develop the power you need to improve athletic skill.

“Whether you’re outside on a run or inside on a skier, rower, or treadmill, alternate going all-out for 20 seconds and resting for 40 to 60 seconds,” says Samuel. Repeat that work-to-rest interval eight to 10 times total.

“The biggest thing to remember when training to become a better athlete is that it’s not about doing lots and lots and lots of work,” says Samuel. “It’s about quality. And if you avoid these four mistakes, you’re going to see yourself get faster, jump higher, and move a lot better on any field of play.”
