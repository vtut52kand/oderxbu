NETFLIX'S THE WATERFRONT is the latest in a series of shows that we can only refer to as Family Empire shows. These are shows where there are two main forces at play: a business (sometimes legitimate, often not), and fraught family dynamics. We can look back to Breaking Bad for this, move into Ozark land, and then get into Yellowstone and Succession too. These are shows where we're meeting characters who have a lot to lose and a lot to maintain, and need to balance it with a level of drama that exists in their own family cell.

Outside of the dynamic of the main characters—where there's often a power struggle that combines with the theme of "blood is thicker than water" love—there's also something these shows often share: single-season villains. Often times, these are characters meant not so much to have an extensive level of depth themselves, but rather to put the main characters in a situation where we're forced to learn more about them. How do they react in a situation when forced to make a tough decision?

The first season of The Waterfront, by that metric, has a fairly brilliant villain in Grady, a drug lord played by Topher Grace. The Waterfront tells the story of the Buckley family, who run a North Carolina fishing empire, and due to their extreme debt, need to resort to drug smuggling through their fishing enterprise. The main players here are father Harlan (Holt McCallany), mother Belle (Maria Bello), son Cane (Jake Weary), and daughter Bree (Melissa Benoist).

Like many patriarch characters in shows like this (think Kevin Costner's John Dutton in Yellowstone and even John Goodman's Eli Gemstone in The Righteous Gemstones), Harlan is hardly a role model, but he lives his life by a very specific moral code. He believes in what he believes in, and he won't often object to that. This is what makes Grady—the young, reckless, drug supplier he comes into contact with midway through the season—such a fantastic villain: These characters are completely opposed to one another. Harlan believes in a process, and doing things the right way; Grady believes in doing whatever makes things easier for him in any given moment, even if that means brutally murdering someone or torturing someone else.

The scenes between McCallany's Harlan and Grace's Grady are also made endlessly entertaining through both the way the characters are written and how the actors choose to perform them. Harlan is a big, strong man who's always serious and stoic about whatever is on his mind. Grady, in contrast, is thin, quick-talking, and constantly making a joke or a mockery of whatever is going on. Even after he kills someone or subjects someone else to an act of torture, he's almost unable to come to terms with the line that he's crossed. He says in a later episode that he's been diagnosed as a narcissistic psychopath, and, well, watching The Waterfront, that's something we can certainly believe.

"We wrote that character with Topher in mind,” the show's creator Kevin Williamson (who wrote Scream, I Know What You Did Last Summer, and more) said in an interview with TVLine. "We wanted someone who was lovable and fun and funny... and to turn them into a psychopath. It’s a lot of fun. Give a funny man a gun and see what he does with it."

Grace is without question a highlight of The Waterfront's first season. The 46-year-old is playing a bit against type—he's still best known for his role as Eric Forman in That '70s Show—but he's certainly dabbled with the sinister before. In Sam Raimi's polarizing Spider-Man 3, Grace played another villain in Eddie Brock/Venom. And while the Venom part of that role may not have been handled the best, Grace's fast-talking Eddie Brock certainly laid the groundwork for what we saw come to life in The Waterfront with Grady.

Even outside of his famous turn as Eddie Brock/Venom in Spider-Man 3, he's subverted his image a number of other times on the big screen—including, perhaps most notably, playing KKK leader David Duke in Spike Lee's BlacKkKlansman. He's also made notable appearances in movies like Interstellar, Under the Silver Lake, and Predators. That's not to say Grace has totally abandoned his 'nice guy' persona either. He's led comedic films like In Good Company and Take Me Home Tonight and played the main character in ABC's underrated sitcom Home Economics.
