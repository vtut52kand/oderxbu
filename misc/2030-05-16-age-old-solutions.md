# Age-Old Solutions

You're primed to party, you look damn good, and you're ready for anything. Then you walk through the door. Your first thought is, "Can't we turn down the music?" And your second thought is, "Oh, God. I'm old." Not only is the music too loud, but you also don't know the song. And you don't look so hot, after all -- you're Lands' End in a sea of Structure. Worst of all, the women scarcely glance your way.

Relax. We all have moments when we feel as if we just got whupped with a fogy stick. They're reminders that we're not teenagers anymore, no matter how many Radiohead songs we've downloaded. But it doesn't have to be that way. Face down those moments of truth and you can feel younger immediately. Here's how.

You enter a room, and you're clueless as to why

Going blank is, in part, a memory "retrieval" problem that comes with growing older. You gradually lose brain neurons as you age, says Gil Einstein, Ph.D., chairman of the psychology department at Furman University. But it's also partially a product of our busy lives. Bob Jacobs, Ph.D., a neuroscientist at Colorado College, points out that cavemen probably met about 150 people in a lifetime, making cocktail-party grunting a breeze. If you blank on someone's name, it's because you're a swamped modern man with a Neanderthal's brain.

How to turn back the clock: Go lefty. Use your nondominant hand for routine things like brushing your teeth, answering the phone, and eating. This stimulates your brain into activating new pathways -- and that means a more nimble mind, says Lawrence C. Katz, Ph.D., a Duke University neurobiologist and coauthor of Keep Your Brain Alive: 83 Neurobic Exercises. Still blanking out? Keep lists. "It helps with processing information," says Margie Lachman, Ph.D., a psychology professor at Brandeis University. Remember (try, anyway) that memory is associative, so if you retrace your steps, there's a good chance you'll recall why you walked into the bedroom. Oh, yeah -- it's bedtime.

You creak in the morning

Cartilage in your joints is wearing thin, and stiffness sets in while you sleep, says Dave Pearson, Ph.D., C.S.C.S., an exercise physiologist at Ball State University. It's the beginnings of osteoarthritis. "The older a hinge is, the better the chance it's going to creak."

How to turn back the clock: Spike your morning coffee with a scoop of NutraJoint Plus Glucosamine, a gelatin-based supplement you can find at your grocery store. Research shows that it relieves pain and may help repair cartilage; it contains nutrients your body turns into cartilage lubricant. But it doesn't work for everyone; if your body is going to respond, you should notice in about 2 months. Taking it now may help forestall arthritis when you're re-ally old, Pearson says. "We spend all this time feeding our muscles, but it's really the joints that sideline us as we age," he says.

You can't pound the beers like you used to

It has nothing to do with age; you're just out of practice, says James Langenbucher, Ph.D., a clinical psychologist at the Center of Alcohol Studies at Rutgers University. Most men steadily drink less after their early 20s. "There's bound to be a decrease in acute alcohol tolerance. You're not living the wild life as much as you used to," says Langenbucher.

How to turn back the clock: Anticipating a big night out? Swing by Wendy's first. The fat and protein in a double burger with cheese will absorb the alcohol. Flirt with the drive-thru girl. If she doesn't gag, it might put a spring in your step.

You just don't get that new video

There's a conspiracy among TV and ad executives to make you feel old. "You are no longer the target of popular culture," says Elaine Wethington, Ph.D., a sociology professor at Cornell University. "You walk into a room, and everything about it is set up for someone with younger tastes."

How to turn back the clock: Tear down your fences, says Mitch Adair, a music-trend forecaster at a firm called Youth Intelligence, in New York City. It's his job to figure out what teenagers will be listening to next. Trouble is, he's already 30. "Stay young at heart," says Adair, who craves a quiet night at home after a week of clubbing. When he is home, his television is pretty much stuck on MTV. He occasionally clicks over to the Independent Film Channel for offbeat flicks, WB for teen shows, and Fox. He reads the music magazines, of course. But mostly he keeps his mind open. "If you're in your 30s, you shouldn't be ornery," he says.

You need a nap

No wonder -- you're not getting as much quality sleep as you used to. When you were 25, about 20 percent of your rack time was deep, slow-wave, restorative sleep. By age 35, you're down to less than 5 percent and fading, according to recent research.

How to turn back the clock: Buy a new a mattress. The one you're sleeping on probably has coils that are hooked together, meaning that a tossing partner (a person moves about 70 times a night) can wake you. This "motion transfer" can ruin up to 20 percent of what little slow-wave sleep you're managing to get, says Jim Maas, Ph.D., a Cornell University psychology professor and author of Power Sleep. A mattress with separate coils -- only Simmons makes them -- can help. So can upgrading to king-size. Either that or make like a 60s sitcom and sleep in separate beds.

You blurt out, "Hey, turn it down!"

"With the aging process, we become more irritable than before," says Stanley Teitelbaum, Ph.D., a New York City psychologist. We're more rigid and "less comfortable with being uncomfortable. We react more to factors like noise and smoke."

How to turn back the clock: Stop covering your ears and start shaking your booty. Physical movement can reduce the stress that makes you think everything is so LOUD, says Raymond Shaw, Ph.D., a professor of psychology at Merrimack College. Warning: Nobody does the hustle anymore.

How to turn back the clock: You can't reverse hearing loss, but you can learn to compensate for it. Stand 3 to 6 feet away from people when you're talking with them. This will make it easier for you to take visual clues from their body language and facial expressions. Remember, too, that hearing loss can be stopped. Always wear ear protection when using power equipment. If you have 10th row, right side for Korn, invest in musician's earplugs from an audiologist. They're molded to your ear and won't distort the music. And you can convince the girl next to you that you're with the band.

The man in the mirror is all wrinkly Wrinkling starts after 30 for most men. Could be a lack of hydration, a breakdown of collagen, a loss of subcutaneous fat. But it's probably the sun, which accounts for almost 90 percent of the appearance of age, says Ronald M. Klatz, M.D., author of Anti-Aging Secrets for Maximum Lifespan.

How to turn back the clock: Two words: moistur-ize. A moisturizer with sodium PCA (most name-brand moisturizers have it) "penetrates the pores, drives hydration in, and maintains it within the skin," Dr. Klatz says. Or grab a tube of alpha-lipoic-acid cream and put it on after you shave. "It can reduce lines, wrinkles, even scars," says Nicholas Perricone, M.D., author of The Wrinkle Cure. Ask your doctor to recommend a brand.

Yesterday's touch-football game left you in a world of hurt

You used to play every weekend; now you play about as often as Navy wins. Sitting in front of a tube all week is bad prep for the "maximum overdrive" that football inspires, Pearson says. Worst are the sudden lunges -- leaping to catch a pass or tag someone--that cause "multiple microscopic tears in connective tissue in the muscle." You don't feel it as it happens. You do feel it during the next 24 to 48 hours. "The first few times you start moving around, all that scar tissue breaks loose and sends a message that you're sore," Pearson says.

How to turn back the clock: Don't wait till the morning after to pop some ibuprofen. Taking it immediately after the game can help lessen the inflammation in your damaged muscles. An even better strategy: Stop laughing at Tae-Bo and try it, Pearson says, "or some other type of aerobics class that would call for those kinds of movements you're not used to making." A regular racquetball or squash game can prepare you for the awkward reaching in football much more than lifting, running, or biking can. And make sure you warm up and cool down thoroughly -- a pulled hamstring is a lot worse than just feeling sore.

You just don't feel like it

"Most men have a decrease in libido around their mid-40s," says Stanley Ducharme, Ph.D., a urologist and sex therapist at the Boston University medical center. Boredom with the same partner, combined with physical changes in the body -- slower arousal, softer erections, less ejaculate -- reinforce that a man is "feeling old, feeling a loss of masculinity."

How to turn back the clock: Find that brochure and book a weekend. No kids. It doesn't have to be a sexathon; just "devote it to tuning in to each other," says Ron Burg, Ph.D., a psychologist at the San Jose Marital and Sexuality Center. Or pick a "date night" to spend the quality time you've missed since the children, jobs, dog, and kitchen redo took over your lives. If the date yields just 30 minutes of touching on deeper subjects, you may be embarked on a comeback, Burg says. "But you have to keep it up -- it's a slow, gradual, cumulative thing."

You decline to bungee-jump You're acting like a baboon -- and almost any other species of mammal, says Robert Sapolsky, Ph.D., a biologist at Stanford University. And that's good. In all animals, there's an aversion to novelty and risk after early adulthood, which shows up in your radio presets (that oldies station) and in your lower car-insurance rates. Nobody's figured out whether it's a change in brain chemistry or a psychosocial thing, but it's universal. Some think that's why longer-living Cro-Magnons survived while Neanderthals died off -- the Neanderthals didn't have the old-timers to apply their wisdom to changes in the environment.

How to turn back the clock: Why bother? You've significantly reduced the chances you'll die like an idiot. Age isn't all bad.
