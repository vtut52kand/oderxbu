# These 6 Muscle-Building Mistakes Are Hurting Your Workout Gains. Here Are the 6 Crucial Fixes.

PEOPLE THINK BUILDING muscle requires only that they show up to the gym consistently and put in any type of strength training work (and guzzle a protein shake here and there) to make their desired gains.

You will need to be willing to put time and effort into your workouts, but muscle building is a science. Like any good science project, there are loads of factors that can make or break your results. Complete those sets and reps submaximally, and you could be costing yourself major gains.

Here, Ebenezer Samuel, C.S.C.S., Men's Health fitness director, talks through the six main mistakes people make when it comes to building muscle—and how to fix them to pack on the size you want.

Don't Only Focus on the Pump

THE "PUMP," AS it's colloquially known, is the feeling of your muscles blowing up when you do a high volume of reps (think about the sensation you get on the tail end of a set of biceps curls). This happens when blood flow starts to increase to the targeted muscle group.

Don't get us wrong, the pump is a great feeling, and you will reap some muscle-building benefits from that type of training. But achieving a pump shouldn't be your only path to muscle. Too many guys chase the pump and feel like they've accomplished everything they can—but there's more work that can be done for optimal muscle building.

DO THIS INSTEAD:

Focus on Intensity and Reps in Reserve.

The level of intensity with which you approach your workouts will be one of the most important factors for muscle-building. "We have to focus on taking our muscles very near their upper limit," Samuel says.

Instead of chasing the pump feeling and stopping when you feel swole, focus instead on your reps in reserve, or the amount of reps you could conceivably perform before failure at the end of the set. Try coming within two reps of failure on every single one of your working sets. Your final rep should be quite a bit more challenging than your early reps. You won't be able to finish so easily, and you might even feel your form start to break down, Samuel says.

That's how far you should be pushing your muscles every single set—even if it means you're only completing five or six reps when you had planned for more. You might not feel the pump, but this strategy will take you much closer to the stimulus you need to build muscle.

Don't Backload Volume

AT SOME POINT in your fitness journey, you've likely made it through a workout and felt as though you didn't work hard enough to burn out. Surely you can handle more—so to compensate, you might have tossed in an extra few unplanned reps or sets to push yourself more. Though this might feel like a good idea, this extra volume is not doing anything but fatiguing you and potentially even setting yourself up for injury.

"I want you train harder in the context of your workout instead," Samuel says.

Add in Drop Sets at the End of Your Workouts.

Instead of adding random extra volume, make the sets you are doing a little bit harder by adding in a drop set at the end of your last exercise of your programmed workout.

Here's what that looks like: Say you have three sets of 10 reps of biceps curls at the end of your upper body day. Once you complete the last set of 10 reps (coming two reps from failure, like we already covered), drop the weight in half, and squeeze out five to six more reps. That will push your muscles closer to failure without sacrificing form the way you might if you're adding in junk volume at your working weight.

Don't Stay in One Rep Range

IT'S A CLASSIC fitness myth: To build muscle, you must do sets of 8 and 12 reps. New science has established that's not the case. Researchers have determined it's possible to build muscle within a wide range of rep schemes, from sets as low as five reps per set all the way up to 30 reps in a set.

You shouldn't do this by crushing 30-rep sets and over-fatiguing your muscles, but there is value in varying your rep schemes for larger return.

Use a Lower Rep Range.

By aiming for only five or six reps, you'll be able to lift heavier and your muscles likely won't be so fatigued for the remainder of your workout.

Take the first exercise in your program (which can also be something you can train with a heavy load), and instead of using the classic three sets of 8 to 12 reps, change that to three to four sets of five reps. That range will help you build strength, power, and facilitate muscle growth.

Don't Focus on Time Spent in the Gym

"ONE OF THE biggest muscle building mistakes happens before you even get to the gym, and it's all about your mindset," Samuel says. "Often, people think it's about the amount of time they spend in the gym, and that's all that matters."

The truth is, time spent in the gym is not the best barometer of a good workout. You might spend three hours a day in there, but if you're busting out some lousy sets at a low intensity then sitting on your phone during rest periods, you're never going to see true change.

Focus on the Intensity of Your Working Sets.

Progress is the result of effort, especially when it comes to building muscle. That means upping your intensity and pushing your muscles as close to their limits as possible without risking injury.

To really ramp up the intensity your workouts, focus on the amount of hard sets you have in a workout rather then the time spent in the gym. Aim to come within two reps of failure on at least six to eight total sets in your session.

"If you're doing that, then you're going to push your muscles a lot harder in terms of intensity. That's what's going to define your workout, whether it takes you 20 minutes or two hours to get there," Samuel says.

Don't Zero Out Cardio

ANOTHER HARMFUL FITNESS misconception: Cardio will hurt your gains. There is some small logic to this idea—when you're aiming to bulk up, you need extra calories to build your muscles. You don't want to burn those extra calories away with cardio, which isn't optimal for building muscle. The problem with this is that if you're not training cardio at all, "you're short circuiting your ability to fight for those last reps," Samuel says.

Taking a strict no-cardio approach can also set yourself up for failure once you're finally done building muscle. You might have achieved the physique you want, but all that mass will just feel heavy when you're cardiovascular system is so neglected that you difficulty with simple tasks like walking up a flight of stairs.

Incorporate Steady-State Cardio a Few Times a Week.

You want to make sure you maintain your cardio at least a little bit. Luckily, that doesn't mean you need to run marathons or load up on burpees. All it takes is a few sessions of steady-state cardio two to three times a week. A simple 15-minute walk or light jog will do the trick.

Light conditioning will also pay off in your strength training practice when you have to dig deep for air to help you power through the final reps in your sets. Plus, you'll thank yourself for not neglecting it the next time you wind up needing to take the stairs.

Don't Over-Focus on Lengthened Partials

YEARS OF EXERCISE science research has shown that growth will come when your muscles are under tension at longer lengths. But some people have taken this to an extreme in practice, with gym-goers performing only half-reps in the lengthened position, a.k.a lengthened partials.

But there are plenty of reasons to continue to train at full range of motion. First, it bulletproofs your strength at every joint angle and muscle position, which will help to prevent injury. Second, it promotes time under tension on every single rep—another key factor for muscle growth.

Stick With Full Range of Motion, and Slow Your Pace.

Instead of worrying about lengthened partials, continue performing full-range reps, but take extra time on the lowering portion of the lift. For example, if you're doing a biceps curl, lift the weights all the way up to the top position, and then aim to take a full two to three seconds to lower them back to your hip. That will work your muscles through those long lengths, while still promoting time under tension and healthy joint strength.
