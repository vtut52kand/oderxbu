By: Mike Magnuson

I ride too much. If you need to reach me on a weekday afternoon, from 11:30 till at least 4:30, don't bother; I'll be riding my bicycle. Don't bother on weekends, either. I'll be away at the races, or if I'm not, I'll be gone most of the day racking up base mileage, extending my endurance, my fitness, improving the intellectual and spiritual connection between my lungs and legs. You might try me in the evening, too, but chances are I'll be so tired from riding I'll be passed out on the couch. Used to be, I smoked two packs a day and passed out on the couch, drunk, after I had bumbled home from the tavern. I was 90 pounds overweight and as miserable as I was fat. Now I'm lean and fit and count my success or failure each week by the number of hours I've spent in the saddle—more than 20, please.

I'm telling you, so much good has happened to me because of cycling, but I've got to stop living like this.

I don't know much, clinically, about addiction, but I don't need to. This would be like requiring a person who loves sunshine to be a trained expert in the scientific function of the sun. It is enough to know the sun is there and that its rays improve the mood. The drunk drinks because booze provides that deep calm where life doesn't hurt so much. Talk to a drunk, talk to a smoker, they'll tell you it just feels natural to do what they do. Talk to me about cycling, I'll tell you I remember riding bikes as a kid, beating around town and jumping curbs and so on. I'll tell you about freedom, the rush of the wind, the feeling of uniqueness and subversion and never needing to do what everybody else does.

So when I got back on my bike at 38 years old, bringing with me an adult lifetime of obesity and compulsions and depression, I did it because cycling was fun, because it was natural, and in no time, I came to believe that, without cycling, I would fall into unspeakable misery. I was no longer seeing each morning as if through the Jim Beam knitting needles poking into my eyes. I was lighter, so I could climb staircases without breaking into a sweat. I didn't smoke, which meant I could breathe better than I ever had, and I could taste the air and smell the flowers and experience a tactile world I had thought gone forever. In a word, life didn't hurt so much anymore.

I am so strong and so happy on the bike. Take that away from me, and I'm not certain I'll have a reason to go on living. It's a trap, I believe, but what else am I going to do?

A few weeks ago, on a Saturday, I tried doing something about it. I was supposed to go off to the races—everybody on my team was going—and my daughter's final soccer game of the season coincided with the race. She's eight years old now, a shy girl, not too confident, and not too assertive, but then again I'm mostly assessing her personality based on the girl she was four years ago. I've been so completely obsessed with cycling I haven't noticed anything else going on around me. It's as if, in my mind, I've been lost on a four-year bender and am only now struggling to snap out of it.

So my daughter played left forward this season on a team called the Wild Dogs, and she took the field that day and committed herself completely to the thick of the action in a brutal 2-2 tie with a team of hard-charging girls called the Chili Peppers. How did my quiet, unassuming daughter turn into that person blasting the ball toward the net and knocking people out of the way with her butt and coming close to yellow cards on a couple of occasions? I have no idea.

By: Bill Powers

Chris called me collect a month before she overdosed on heroin.

My sister and her boyfriend, another junkie from the squatter's camp in San Diego, were Greyhound-ing it to his parent's house in Oregon. Could we grab a coffee as they passed through Los Angeles and, if it wasn't too much trouble, would it be cool for them to shower at my place since they'd be sleeping on Venice Beach? I said no, that theirs sounded like a bad plan. And that I didn't think I could meet right now. My step-father had just died from brain cancer and seeing my sister strung out, homeless, was simply too much for me to handle. At the time, my response felt like self-preservation, setting boundaries. Later I cursed myself for denying her simple request. Before we hung up though, Chris told me she loved me and I reciprocated, begging her to get clean again. A month later, the cops found her lifeless body near an overrun ditch off the 405 Expressway. Chris was 33 years old and several weeks pregnant.

People will give you a lot of sage tips about how to deal with an active addict, but there is no uniform strategy that guarantees success. The truth is, if you experience enough relapses with them, then you will go through the complete roster of possible reactions: caretaking, rage, indifference, disbelief, everything. Sometimes I would wire money when Chris rang in dire need, other times I offered unsolicited advice instead. Personally, I never subscribed to the popular tough love approach, because we tend to forget entirely about the love part, and I feared that being a hard-ass would only make Chris feel like no one cared anymore. Addicts suspect, especially given their egregious behavior, that even unconditional love will have its limits.

The temptation with an addict in the family, of course, is to claim their injuries are purely self-inflicted. Ultimately aren't we are all responsible for our own decisions? It's a sad fact that too often we witness a person's worst instincts manifested and believe these actions to be their most sincere.

In my sister's case, the real wounds went back to 1980, after our parent's divorce, when mom—newly liberated—dragged us through her messy second adolescence. We lived on a commune in Southampton, a place where narcotics were rampant and Chris was molested. Once we took a family vacation to Club Med(itation) in The Bahamas. Mom broke out the magic mushrooms for the night we got mantras from this famous yogi visiting the ashram. I was too scared to trip, but Chris, who was 13 years old, said yes. I don't think my sister overcame the wreckage from such negligence. When mom sobered up in the mid-80s, she did her best to make living amends to both of us, but the emotional baggage was enough to fill an airbus cargo hold.

Chris's situation improved steadily after she began living under my father's roof. There was tennis camp, part-time work at the local florist and trips to Europe. His was a very stable household. Chris even attended a private girl's school on the Upper East Side until she showed up to homeroom one morning with a Mohawk and booze on her breath. After a failed suicide attempt and a stint in Minnesota, there was another stab at graduating, but with 18 looming on the calendar, no one could prevent Chris from bolting.

Twelve Step programs will tell you that sobriety isn't for the people who need it, but rather the people who want it. I tried to help manufacture the necessary desire for my sister to stop using—mostly leading by example—but I never imagined drugs would actually kill her. Chris was New York street smart and truck-driver tough. This was the girl who in 10th grade took her braces off with a pair of pliers, because she wanted to go nightclubbing with her friends; the girl who moved to Hawaii with a one-way plane ticket and pocket change. Unfortunately, all the drama she brought to our lives: the school suspensions, abusive boyfriends, passing out under the Christmas tree, each one of these unfolding "emergencies" and Chris's ability to survive them, well, they eventually desensitized me to the seriousness of her life and death struggle.

I have learned that a person's chances of recovering are not always proportional to the level of damage they've sustained. I have seen stone-cold junkies sober 15 years strong and society girls, the kind Chris went to high school with at Hewitt, who appear doomed forever to relapse.

Maybe it's because my mother was a shrink and, in my youth, a total hypocrite that I still bristle at so many self-help terms. They seem so Stuart Smalley, fake-sounding. And words like "enabler" have always rubbed me the wrong way. Worrying excessively about whether you are enabling a person you love bears little fruit. Better to employ your common sense.

For instance, at the last rehab my sister checked out of against medical advice, the counselors recommended to my parents that they not to speak to Chris until she had a year of continuous sobriety and was fully self-supporting. Neither one of them could maintain vigilant radio silence, and they spoke to Chris through the distorted veil of long distance until her final days. I think they are both glad they didn't heed the professional's advice. I have yet to meet a person who regretted assisting their addicted kid get back on their feet, as long as they didn't help them to run.
