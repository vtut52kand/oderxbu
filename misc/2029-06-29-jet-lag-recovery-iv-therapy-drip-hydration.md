Why Jet Lag Takes a Toll

When you cross multiple time zones in a short period, your body’s internal clock—or circadian rhythm—gets out of sync with the local day-night cycle. This disruption doesn’t just affect sleep; it can cause fatigue, irritability, difficulty concentrating, and general discomfort. Jet lag is recognized as a circadian rhythm sleep disorder, and symptoms usually begin within hours of arrival and can last for days.

Airplane travel itself can make the effects worse. In addition to time zone changes, flights expose your body to pressurized, dry air with low humidity, which can lead to dehydration. Dehydration during travel can worsen feelings of fatigue, reduce cognitive performance, and disrupt your body’s ability to regulate sleep hormones like melatonin—further amplifying jet lag symptoms.

Beyond disrupted sleep and fatigue, dehydration and nutrient depletion during travel can impair your overall wellness. Electrolytes like sodium, potassium, and magnesium are lost due to dry cabin air and limited fluid intake, contributing to muscle cramps, headaches, and feelings of general malaise.
