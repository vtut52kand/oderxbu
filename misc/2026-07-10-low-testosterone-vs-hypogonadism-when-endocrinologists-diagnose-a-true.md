Low Testosterone vs. Hypogonadism: When Endocrinologists Diagnose a True Hormonal Disorder

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Endocrinologists diagnose male hypogonadism—not normal age-related testosterone decline—when two specific criteria are met: persistent symptoms (low libido, fatigue, muscle loss, mood changes) AND consistently low early-morning total testosterone confirmed on at least two separate blood tests. Additional hormone panels (LH, FSH, prolactin) help classify the condition as primary (testicular failure) or secondary (pituitary or hypothalamic origin), which directly guides treatment.

Key diagnostic requirements:

Symptoms persisting for weeks or months, not transient

Morning testosterone (drawn 7–10 a.m.) below lab reference range, confirmed twice

Follow-up labs to identify the underlying cause

Imaging or specialist referral when secondary causes are suspected

Treatment varies based on lifestyle factors, fertility goals, and underlying cause—so understanding your specific picture matters before pursuing therapy.

Because low testosterone shares symptoms with thyroid disorders, depression, sleep apnea, and other conditions, self-diagnosis often sends people down the wrong path—wasting months and money on the wrong treatment. Explanation

Testosterone is a key hormone for men's health, affecting energy, mood, muscle mass, bone density and sexual function. It's not uncommon for adult men to experience lower testosterone levels as they age. But how do you know when "low T" crosses the line into a genuine medical condition called male hypogonadism? Endocrinologists use specific criteria—symptoms, lab tests and sometimes imaging—to make that call. Understanding the difference can help you take the right steps toward diagnosis and treatment.

What Is Testosterone and Why It Matters

Testosterone is produced mainly in the testes under stimulation by hormones from the pituitary gland. It plays a central role in:

Libido (sex drive) and erectile function

Muscle mass, strength and body composition

Bone density and fracture prevention

Mood regulation, energy levels and confidence

Fat distribution and metabolic health

When testosterone levels drop below the normal range, you may notice changes in these areas. But not every man with a slightly low lab result has true male hypogonadism.

Defining Male Hypogonadism

Male hypogonadism is a clinical syndrome characterized by:

Symptoms or signs of testosterone deficiency

Consistently low testosterone levels on blood tests

There are two main types:

Primary hypogonadism: Testicular failure leads to low testosterone. Common causes include genetic conditions (e.g., Klinefelter syndrome), testicular injury, chemotherapy or infection.

Secondary hypogonadism: The pituitary gland or hypothalamus fails to stimulate the testes. Causes can include pituitary tumors, certain medications, obesity or chronic illness.

When hypogonadism begins later in life—usually after age 40—it's sometimes called Late Onset Hypogonadism (LOH).

Low Testosterone vs. Hypogonadism: Spotting the Difference

Many factors can temporarily lower testosterone—stress, poor sleep, obesity, alcohol use or medications. A single low reading doesn't automatically mean hypogonadism. Endocrinologists look for a pattern:

Symptoms: Persistent fatigue, low libido, erectile dysfunction, depressed mood or loss of muscle mass

When to See an Endocrinologist

Persistent symptoms despite lifestyle changes

Multiple early-morning labs showing low testosterone

Suspected pituitary or testicular pathology

Concerns about fertility or long-term health risks (bone density, cardiovascular)

An endocrinologist specializes in hormone disorders and can guide you through diagnosis, testing and treatment.

Important Warning: Talk to Your Doctor

While many men experience a subtle decline in testosterone with age, some symptoms overlap with life-threatening conditions such as heart disease, diabetes or malignancy. If you experience any of the following, seek medical attention promptly:

Chest pain, shortness of breath or heart palpitations

Unexplained weight loss or night sweats

Conclusion

Distinguishing simple age-related decreases in testosterone from true male hypogonadism requires a careful blend of symptom assessment, repeat blood tests and specialist evaluation. While lifestyle factors often play a role, persistent signs combined with consistently low morning testosterone levels point toward a hormone disorder that may benefit from targeted treatment.
