# I’m a Fitness Trainer. Here’s How We Can Make a Presidential Fitness Test Work for Kids.

EARLIER THIS YEAR, the Trump Administration announced the reinstatement of The Presidential Physical Fitness Test, the long-running program that assessed youth health and fitness from 1966 to 2013. When I was in school, I looked forward to the test every year. It gave us a venue to strive to be among the strongest, fastest, and most athletic kids in class. I remember sprinting to pick up chalkboard erasers and pumping through situps as fast as possible in my quest to earn a blue patch as one of the top performers. Now, I'm a personal trainer with over 15 years of experience, and I've worked with everyone from children to senior citizens, high performers and beginners. (I'm also a parent, too.)

[We Can Make Presidential](https://tissue-renewal.china-akan.com/)

The program was a key motivator for me as a kid. But the test also had flaws: it lacked movement education of any kind, results were measured for time like an elementary CrossFit competition, and, ultimately, the kids who couldn’t perform were isolated from those who could. Those issues were largely why it was replaced in 2013 with Fitnessgram, another fitness test, this one more research-based. That also didn’t work. Why? Because the root problem, teaching kids the exercises they had to do, wasn’t addressed. Children were left flailing through pullups and jogging only fast enough to pass their test.

Forget for a moment why these programs failed. Imperfect as they were, they were attempts to address the youth physical activity problem that's taken hold in the United States. Point blank, kids don't move enough. The 2024 U.S. Report Card on Physical Activity for Children and Youth gave the country a D-. Less than 28% of children between the ages 6 and 17 are getting the recommended 60 minutes of daily activity. Break those numbers down further, and it looks worse: Less than 50 percent of younger kids (ages 6–11) hit that mark, and barely 15 percent of adolescents do. Long term, this is leaving us all more physically fragile: The lack of movement will eventually leave young bodies less physically equipped to withstand the rigors of adulthood and – even worse – with less of a reservoir of muscle to draw from as they age.

The Presidential Physical Fitness Test at least puts fitness back into the spotlight of public consciousness of youngsters, even if only for a moment, as we have a national debate over its merits. Suddenly, fitness is front-and-center for youth, because they know they have to take the test. But the test has to meet them where they are, too. Here’s how we need to tweak it.

Why Schools Need to Test—and Make Fitness a Bigger Priority

The reason I care about this isn’t because I want to see who can run the fastest mile or do the most pushups. It’s because testing (when done right) provides a measurable way to track a child’s progress over time.

Think about it: We have in-depth ways of tracking mathematical progress and expectations about how students will improve from year to year. We have similar measures for reading skills. But for physical education? When I was in school, we never had the same focus on following a curriculum and testing concepts or mastery of those concepts. The Presidential Physical Fitness Test gives us a way to treat phys. ed like any other subject in school. This is an exam, and now you're at least studying (or not studying) for something you can work to pass.

If all we do is test our children's brains (and not their bodies) in school, we're limiting how we can affect their growth (and maybe costing them the cognitive benefits of movement, too). Thing is, you can’t test youngsters in something unless you prep them for it. If we’re planning to test children on pullups or pushups, we need to teach them how to do those moves. Teachers wouldn’t give pop quizzes on calculus without spending days teaching and assigning homework. Ditto squats and mile-long runs.

How do we do that? We provide children with activity guides for their time outside of school and create in-school opportunities for them to practice their exercises. Other school subjects have homework and give parents guidance for the summer. If we support children’s brains this way, we need to support their physical health, too.

None of this will make the testing procedure easier, though. Physical fitness tests are notorious for highlighting the shortcomings of less capable children as they struggle to complete tasks in public. This is also somewhat unavoidable: Unlike, say, science class, you can’t simply give a student a test that can be graded later and handed back in private. Passing or failing a fitness test is obvious in the moment.

Then again, all tests have their failings. And when it comes to physical fitness tests, we do have some options. One fix: Encourage children to work together instead of facing off as individuals. Another: Testing in smaller groups, where everyone focuses on their own task. Overall, creating an environment in which children cheer for and encourage each other is key. And sure, that might be idealistic – but it’s an ideal worth chasing.

The Path Forward

Back to the Presidential Physical Fitness Test. Despite its holes, it gives us at least a start on testing fitness. Our goal going forward should be to surround it with infrastructure and create other opportunities for children to express their physicality (and be rewarded for it).

Progress over Performance: Like all tests, sometimes the final grade isn't everything. Often, it's about showing gradual improvement. An app or website for parents and children can help with this. No, a kid may not be able to crush 5 pushups in first grade, but if he's progressed to three pushups a year later, that progress matters and should be celebrated.

Lead with Knowledge: Physical education teachers need to be certified in coaching exercises and movements that have been identified as essential for progress. Not all kids will be active in sports. For those who aren’t, there should be enough support supplied in classrooms to teach children the fundamentals of exercise and set movement goals after school.

Bringing back the Presidential Physical Fitness Test only means something if we use it to address the core issue: a decaying prioritization of the health of our children. Measuring the health and fitness of our youth is a step we need to take if we plan to give the next generation a healthier outcome than we currently have. It’s about creating a system that gives kids the tools to be healthy, confident, and capable for the rest of their lives.
