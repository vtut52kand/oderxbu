Aortic Stenosis: The 3 Symptoms That Tell Doctors the Valve Is Getting Critical

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Severe aortic stenosis is a serious heart condition where the aortic valve narrows critically, restricting blood flow from the heart to the rest of the body. The three hallmark warning signs of severe aortic stenosis are:

Chest pain or tightness during exertion (angina)

Shortness of breath, even at rest or with mild activity

Dizziness, lightheadedness, or fainting (syncope)

Recognizing these symptoms early is critical. Untreated severe aortic stenosis can rapidly progress to heart failure, cardiac arrest, or sudden death. Treatment decisions—guided by symptom severity, echocardiogram findings, and overall heart function—may include valve replacement procedures such as TAVR or surgical AVR.

Explanation

Aortic stenosis (AS) occurs when the aortic valve in your heart narrows, forcing your heart to work harder to pump blood into the body. Early on, you may not notice anything wrong, but as the valve tightens, certain aortic stenosis symptoms become hard to ignore. Recognizing these warning signs can make the difference between timely treatment and serious complications.

Why Early Detection Matters

Aortic stenosis often worsens gradually over years.

Once it reaches a "critical" stage, the risks of heart failure, irregular heartbeats and sudden cardiac events rise sharply.

Treating severe AS promptly—sometimes with valve replacement—improves symptoms and survival.

Other Common Aortic Stenosis Symptoms

While the three above signal critical narrowing, you may notice additional signs as AS progresses:

Fatigue – Ongoing tiredness due to limited cardiac output.

Heart Murmur – A doctor's stethoscope may detect a harsh, rasping sound as blood squirts through the tight valve.

Palpitations – Awareness of a racing, pounding or irregular heartbeat.

Swollen Ankles or Feet – Fluid retention from a struggling heart.

If you experience any combination of these symptoms, especially the big three—chest pain, shortness of breath or fainting—prompt evaluation is essential.

What "Critical" Aortic Stenosis Means

Doctors use echocardiograms and other tests to measure valve opening size, pressure gradients and heart function. Critical AS often shows:

Valve opening area ≤ 0.8 cm² (normal is 3–4 cm²)

Peak pressure gradient ≥ 64 mmHg across the valve

Symptoms that limit daily activities

At this stage, the risk of complications—heart failure, sudden cardiac death, severe arrhythmias—rises quickly. Timely referral to a cardiologist and discussion of treatment options (like valve repair or replacement) can reverse or stabilize symptoms.

When to Seek Medical Help

Don't ignore warning signs. Contact a healthcare professional if you experience:

Persistent or worsening chest pain

Increasing shortness of breath, even at rest

Any episodes of fainting or near-fainting

New or worsening fatigue and palpitations

Treatment Options

Treatment depends on severity, symptoms and overall health:

For mild or moderate AS without symptoms

Regular follow-up and echocardiograms

Control blood pressure, manage heart rhythm and fluid retention

Note: No drugs reverse the narrowing itself

Surgical Aortic Valve Replacement (SAVR)

Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement (TAVR) for those at higher surgical risk

Moderate exercise (as advised by your cardiologist)

Heart-healthy diet

Sodium restriction if fluid buildup is an issue

Discuss risks and benefits with your doctor. When symptoms of critical AS appear, valve replacement often offers the best outcome.

Living with Aortic Stenosis

Stay active within tolerated limits.

Keep medical appointments and echo schedules.

Watch for changes in your aortic stenosis symptoms.

Adhere to prescribed medications.

Discuss when it's safe to exercise, travel or undergo other procedures (e.g., dental work with antibiotic prophylaxis if recommended).
