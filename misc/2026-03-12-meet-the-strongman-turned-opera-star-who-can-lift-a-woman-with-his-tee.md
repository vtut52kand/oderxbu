# Meet the Strongman Turned Opera Star Who Can Lift a Woman With His Teeth

Titano Oddfellow, 45, is a professional sideshow performer who is appearing in the Metropolitan Opera's current production of Mozart's Cosi Fan Tutte

[Star Who Can Lift](https://tendonforge.dns0663.com/)

He holds multiple strongman records, including one for "Heaviest Person Lifted Using Teeth" and "Fastest Time To Bend Five Screwdrivers With Bare Hands"

He swears he doesn't work out and that his training is largely "mind over matter"

Titano Oddfellow doesn't even lift, bro. Not weights, anyway. In lieu of hitting the weight room, the 45-year-old sideshow performer will pick up the occasional audience member with his teeth, in between pounding scissors into his nose with sledge hammers and driving nails through wooden boards with his bare hands. Instead of having a vigorous cross-training regimen or sticking to a strict high-protein diet, a lot of it, he says simply, is "mind over matter."

Whatever he's doing, it's paying off. Oddfellow's showmanship and feats of strength have taken him from playing dive bars to opening for bands to center stage at the famed Metropolitan Opera House, where he's currently appearing in a Coney Island-themed production of Mozart's Cosi Fan Tutte (ends Apr. 19) and has the distinction of being the only Metropolitan Opera performer to hold the record for “Fastest Time to Crush Five Cooking Pots While Swallowing a Sword.”

So how does one become a full-time professional strongman? As unlikely as it might seem, Oddfellow says his super-strength is the result of sheer genetics. Growing up in the Midwest, he "always had [some] kind of had farm boy strength or something. I was naturally strong," he told MensHealth.com.

Still, the idea of pursuing a career based on strength never even occurred to him. He was a bike messenger and itinerant worker, spending his 20s riding the rails and doing odd jobs, when a friend encouraged him to give being a strongman a try.

At first, Oddfellow demurred, thinking that he was too gentle to fit into the strongman stereotype. "I'm just, like, so not macho," he says. "I didn't want to do it."

Eventually, he gave in and agreed just to try it just once, in front of 300 people in a Milwaukee beer hall. His initial idea was to "wear some gym shorts and rip a phone book in half," but his sense of showmanship led him to focus more on developing an actual strongman character, "I was like, 'I got way more imagination than that,'" he recalls. "So I just spent a week and made my whole getup." The result was the character Titano Oddfellow: part gladiator, part Viking, and all brute strength.

With variety acts, however, looking the part is not enough. Training to become a strongman (or woman) is less about exercise-based strength training and more about mastering time-honored skills through repetition. As he explains it, “each thing I do as a feat of strength is either something dreamed up by me, or recreated from seeing pictures from a long-gone era of strong person feats of strength and showpersonship.”

For example, one of the first feats he mastered is known as the “human hammer,” or driving a nail through a board with one bare hand. For Titano, it took a solid two or three weeks of diligent practice in his backyard to get the skill down before moving on to two boards, then a frying pan, then two boards and a frying pan.

"I want people to find a place where they can tap into something that's more than muscle."

Now a full-time performer, Oddfellow still considers himself more rock star than gym rat, avoiding the daily grind of the gym and power smoothies. "I don't have a workout routine," he says, "because I suffer from that thing called 'free-spriritedness' that most artistic people have." He views himself less as a strongman in the vein of the Mountain and more of a "clown" for his audience.

"I want to show people more than someone who works out all the time," he says. He does this by creating a character that audiences can relate to rather than being intimidating — no small feat for a guy in a horned headdress with facial tattoos.

Since Oddfellow is such a unique case, it goes without saying that his methods wouldn't work for most people. But even if he doesn't think of himself as a fitness role model (nor does he in any way wish to encourage people to try his feats of strength at home), he wants his audience to view him as inspirational regardless.

"I want to inspire people to look into the other part of themselves, where it's mind over matter," he says. "I want people to find a place where they can tap into something that's more than muscle. Everyone has that spirit inside of them, so to speak."
