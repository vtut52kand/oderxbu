Long QT syndrome (LQTS) is a rare heart rhythm disorder in which delayed electrical recovery between heartbeats causes a prolonged QT interval on an ECG. This delay can trigger dangerous arrhythmias such as torsades de pointes or ventricular fibrillation, potentially leading to fainting, seizures, or sudden cardiac arrest.

How is Long QT syndrome diagnosed? Doctors use resting and exercise ECGs, Holter monitoring, genetic testing, and electrolyte evaluation to confirm LQTS.

How is Long QT syndrome treated? Treatment includes beta blockers, lifestyle modifications, and implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) for high-risk patients.

Explanation

Long QT syndrome is a heart rhythm condition that affects the heart's electrical system. Though it's relatively rare, understanding long QT syndrome is crucial because it can lead to life-threatening arrhythmias if left undetected. Below, we explain what long QT syndrome is, why it matters, how it's diagnosed, and what you can do to stay safe.

What Is Long QT Syndrome?

Long QT syndrome (LQTS) occurs when the heart's electrical system takes too long to "recharge" between beats. This delay is visible on an electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG) as a prolonged QT interval. In simple terms:

QT interval: The time it takes for the heart's lower chambers (ventricles) to depolarize (contract) and then repolarize (reset) before the next beat.

Prolongation: When this reset phase is longer than normal, it can predispose the heart to dangerous rhythms.

Types of Long QT Syndrome

Congenital (inherited) LQTS

Caused by genetic mutations affecting ion channels in heart cells.

Often presents in childhood or adolescence but can go unnoticed until adulthood.

Acquired LQTS

Triggered by certain medications, electrolyte imbalances (low potassium, magnesium), or medical conditions.

Reversible once the underlying cause is corrected.

Common Symptoms and Warning Signs

Many individuals with long QT syndrome are asymptomatic until a serious event occurs. When symptoms do appear, they often include:

Palpitations (feeling of a racing or pounding heart)

Dizziness or lightheadedness

Fainting spells (often triggered by exercise, stress, or sudden noises)

Seizures (resulting from reduced blood flow to the brain)

How Long QT Syndrome Is Detected

Detecting long QT syndrome relies on a combination of history-taking, physical examination, and specific tests:

1. Electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG)

Resting ECG: Measures the QT interval at rest.

Exercise ECG: Assesses how the QT interval changes with exertion—some forms of inherited LQTS only appear during exercise.

Interpreting the QT interval takes into account heart rate, age, and sex. A corrected QT interval (QTc) longer than 450 ms in men or 460 ms in women often raises concern.

2. Holter Monitor (24- to 48-Hour ECG)

Records heart rhythm over a longer period.

Captures episodic QT prolongation or arrhythmias that a short ECG might miss.

3. Stress Test

Monitors heart rhythm while you exercise on a treadmill or stationary bike.

Helps identify exercise-triggered QT prolongation.

4. Genetic Testing

Identifies mutations in genes known to cause congenital LQTS (e.g., KCNQ1, KCNH2, SCN5A).

Guides family screening—relatives may also carry the same mutation.

5. Blood Tests

Check electrolyte levels (potassium, magnesium, calcium).

Rule out reversible causes of QT prolongation, such as drug interactions.

Managing and Treating Long QT Syndrome

Once diagnosed, treatment focuses on preventing dangerous arrhythmias and managing triggers.

Lifestyle and Trigger Avoidance

Avoid strenuous exercise or sudden loud noises if you have exercise-induced LQTS.

Steer clear of medications known to prolong the QT interval (ask your doctor or pharmacist).

Keep electrolytes balanced—maintain adequate potassium and magnesium through diet or supplements if recommended.

Medications

Beta-blockers (e.g., propranolol, nadolol)Reduce heart rate and the risk of arrhythmias.

Sodium channel blockers (in specific types of LQTS)May help normalize electrical activity in the heart.

Devices and Procedures

Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD)Delivers an electric shock to restore normal rhythm if a life-threatening arrhythmia occurs.

Left cardiac sympathetic denervation (LCSD)A surgical procedure that reduces the risk of arrhythmias by disrupting certain nerve pathways.

Living with Long QT Syndrome

A diagnosis of long QT syndrome often raises concerns, but many people lead full, active lives with proper management. Key steps include:

Regular follow-up with a cardiologist specializing in heart rhythm disorders.

Informing schools, coaches, or employers about your condition and any emergency plan.

Using medical ID bracelets or alert cards to guide first responders if an event occurs.

Encouraging family members to get screened, since LQTS can run in families.

When to Seek Immediate Medical Attention

If you or someone you know with long QT syndrome experiences:

Sudden collapse or loss of consciousness

Palpitations accompanied by chest pain or extreme shortness of breath

Seizure-like activity not previously diagnosed as epilepsy

…call emergency services right away.
