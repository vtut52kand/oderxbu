# 'Will & Harper' Demands More of Will Ferrell (and the Rest of Us)

WILL FERRELL ISN'T a performer viewers associate with tears. Not fake tears, though he’s squirted a few of those every once in a while for his work, and especially not real tears. He’s naturally irreverent. Lachrymosity is for the other guys. (Incidentally, the movie The Other Guys is one where Ferrell’s character does, in fact, weep.) Placed in mortal peril, and given the choice of either making one last gag or exhibiting sentiment, he’d take the gag. He is a constitutionally unserious human being, in the eyes of his audience.

Josh Greenbaum’s new documentary, Will & Harper (which is now streaming on Netflix), bluntly, but not brusquely, reveals a side of the legendary Saturday Night Live alum we don’t see in his film roles: The side that cares so much about his best friend that he’d embark on a sixteen day cross-country road trip to explore their bond in light of life-changing developments, and the side that’s so distressed by failing to protect his friend that he breaks down crying. In the doc’s context, the friend is Harper Steele, Ferrell’s old chum from their early SNL days; the development is her gender transition. The trip is Ferrell’s idea, an homage to Steele’s love of the open road and her time spent driving cross country solo in her younger days.

But it’s 2024, and though a number of U.S. regions have flipped their attitudes toward transgender Americans, its middle and southern reaches remain dangerous ground for them to tread. Steele came out to her friends via email in 2021, as the U.S. slowly shook off its Covid restrictions and lockdowns; Ferrell was in Boston at the time, shooting the Christmas musical comedy Spirited. Seeing one another again for the first time in years would have been an occasion whatever additional circumstances came packaged with it. Steele’s transition casts a bright light on their post-Covid reunion, as well as on their relationship; Ferrell had questions he knew he had to ask Steele, not to satisfy his own curiosity but because it was incumbent on him, as Steele’s longtime friend, to do so.

The work of being an ally to the trans people in your life is hard, and can occasionally be awkward. It’s also a two way-street; Ferrell has thoughts for Steele, and Steele likewise has thoughts for Ferrell. “What does being trans mean to him?” Steele says in the B-roll in the movie’s opening. “Does he think I’m a woman? Does he know any trans people besides me?” The camera cuts to Ferrell: “Why the name ‘Harper’? How long did she feel this way? What made her keep this in for so long?” Steele wonders if Ferrell will ask her “all the questions you’re not supposed to ask trans people,” which segues to the film’s first big punchline: “Does she still like shitty beer, or is she just totally into wine now?” Ferrell asks the camera. Cut to Steele grousing, “Why does he think Natty Lite is a terrible beer? It’s a fucking delicious beer! It’s the best beer out there!”

The weight of a conversation about self-identification and gender transition aside, neither Ferrell nor Steele tackle the film’s exercise in a wholly sober spirit; this is as key to their individual essences as to their professional partnership. They have fun. They can’t help it! It’s in their blood. But “fun” complicates matters every now and again by muddling the trip’s goal, which is reaffirming a friendship that’s nearly three decades running. Ferrell wants to be Steele’s buddy, advocate, and ally. Will & Harper frames the platonic intimacy fostered by the car ride as self-education; by spending concentrated time with Steele, Ferrell will learn to be those things in two weeks and some change. This is good, poignant, and noble, and it’s what cis people must commit themselves to if they wish to support their trans friends, family, and neighbors.

Good intentions, however, have a way of revealing blind spots and ignorance in the moments where allyship is needed most. Steele and Ferrell attend a Pacers game and unexpectedly meet Indiana Governor Eric Holcomb. It’s a pleasant enough encounter that sours immediately after, when they Google his stance on transgender people and find that he banned gender-affirming care in the state. “I wish I’d had the wherewithal to go, ‘What’s your stance on trans people?’” Ferrell tells Steele the following day. Steele, in kind, recognizes how commonplace perfunctory interactions like the exchange with Holcomb for Ferrell are: “This must happen to you a lot—the photo you don’t wanna be in.”

This is, admittedly, specific to Ferrell’s fame. Most of us won’t be ambushed at public events by elected officials prowling for PR style points. But the substance of what Ferrell wrestles with in the rearview is universal. Given the chance to speak truth to power, he didn’t, and the regret he feels for not doing so is palpable. Steele doesn’t hold it against him; Ferrell is learning. He’s aware that his celebrity functions as both a barrier and a magnet. In almost any social solar system he orbits, Ferrell is the affable goofus at the center, whether he wants to be or not. Attention that falls on him consequently falls on Steele, too. For her, this is a double-edged sword. She wants to be able to go out in public and be seen: she doesn’t want to be noticed, which is another sensation entirely.

Ferrell has a much looser grasp of the difference between the two. Combined with his lived experience as a cisgender, heterosexual man—a cisgender, heterosexual man who clearly believes trans people exist, and who seems not to have a bigoted bone in his body, but a cishet man nonetheless—his nescience is a liability. Ferrell puts on his Sherlock Holmes costume, which he held onto after wrapping 2018’s Holmes & Watson, to grab a 72-ounce sirloin at an Amarillo steakhouse, which is like dousing a grease fire with water; every soul in the room knows they’re there. Unlike the Oklahoma dive bar adorned with “Fuck Joe Biden” flags, whose patrons gave Steele a warm, welcoming, even loving reception, the steakhouse is tense.

It does not escape Steele and Ferrell’s attention that she’s being noticed. A cursory Twitter search spills a flood of anonymous transphobic vitriol over the next day’s drive; everything is bigger in Texas, including hate. Ferrell sobs in the passenger seat. He feels responsible for the online transphobic attacks on Harper, and for the possibility of putting her in real danger. Harper, again, does not hold this against him. But Ferrell holds it against himself, and the film’s refusal to end this particular chapter on an upbeat note is our cue not to let him off the hook, either. There is no judgment in the edit, but there is an object lesson in how drastically allyship can take a perilous turn when we neglect the potentiality of transphobic violence. The moment that Ferrell makes his showmanship the focal point, he is suddenly, frighteningly reminded of the cultural and political forces arrayed against trans people, and Steele.

Ferrell doesn’t make the same mistake twice, and though further tears are shed as Will & Harper closes in on its finish line, what prevails is joy. Ultimately, this is a film about best friends being pals, lightheartedly unpacking a significant matter with respect but little reverence. Ferrell, in a recurring gag, pleads to Steele, like a child to a parent, for a Dunkin’ Donuts pit stop whenever they pass by one. “We’ve not done one fun thing! Name one fun thing we’ve done!” he whines as Steele reflexively cackles. Just minutes earlier, they were floating over Albuquerque in a hot air balloon with Will Forte; twenty minutes later, they’re shooting off fireworks in the desert near Trona, California, where Steele owns a home she purchased after she started to transition. They’re having fun. It’s a necessary supply for their trip, core to Will & Harper’s heart. But the substance is the somber realization of true allyship’s responsibilities, and the effort it takes to bear them.
