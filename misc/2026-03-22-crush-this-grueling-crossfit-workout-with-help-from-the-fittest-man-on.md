Mat Fraser is never satisfied. A few days ago, the two-time defending CrossFit Games champ and reigning "Fittest Man on Earth" set a CrossFit Open record with workout 18.4, a punishing dyad of deadlifts and handstands. Dozens of men in the top 100 failed to finish within the 9-minute time cap. Fraser finished in 6:02.

But when Fraser reviewed his video submission, he still wasn't happy.

"I know all my reps were good," says Fraser, who appears in the CrossFit documentary The Redeemed and the Dominant: Fittest on Earth, which is now available for streaming. "But with the camera angle and all that — I just wasn't happy with how it looked, so I redid it a few hours later." By the end of the day, Fraser had deadlifted 180 reps (for a total of 48,600 pounds), thrown out his world record, and posted a new score of 6:36 (good enough for fifth place), all in order to meet his own standard: perfection.

At the past two CrossFit Games, Fraser led by such a wide margin that he claimed victory before the last event even ended, and his 18.4 performance suggests that he has no intention of slowing down. That’s why we thought he was the perfect person to guide us through the latest (and final) workout of the CrossFit Open, 18.5, a seven-minute, lung-submerging sprint of thrusters and chest-to-bar pull-ups.

How to Do It:

1. Rest when you want to, not when you have to

For the average athlete, Fraser recommends breaking up the work often with short breaks. When you're working at max effort, seven minutes is a long time, and even the elite athletes don't go unbroken. The key, says Fraser, is knowing the pace at which you can keep working while keeping your heart beating at a sustainable rate. Before you start 18.5, experiment with how many pull-ups and thrusters you can do without redlining, and don't blow past that number once the workout begins.

2. Know where in the movement to catch your breath

When you're on the thrusters, you'll want to hyperventilate from start to finish — or worse yet, forget to breathe entirely — but timing is crucial here. According to Fraser, the best time to breathe is at the top of the movement, when the barbell is overhead. If that sounds more exhausting than it is restorative, you might need to correct your form. "For me, it's just finding that good stacked position where your bones and joints are holding the weight, not your muscles," says Fraser. "It's obviously not weightless, but you aren't straining to hold it up."

3. Drop the hammer at the end
