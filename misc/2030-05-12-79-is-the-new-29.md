# 79 Is The New 29

Mr. Ripped is marching up the trail. There is only one rule on these hikes: Do not pass Mr. Ripped. It’s a good rule, because attempting to pass him could put you at risk of a cardiac event. His pace is brisk, almost a jog. The grade is steep, the air thin. We’re hammering a trail at 6,000 feet in the Sandia Mountains near his home in Albuquerque. Mr. Ripped, whose real name is Clarence Bass, does this hourlong hiking circuit once a week, but it’s enough—just enough, combined with one weekly weightlifting session and walking and staying active every day—to place him among the fittest men in America, a physical specimen par excellence who, at 79, may be unrivaled in his age group anywhere in the world.

You’d be forgiven if you’ve never heard of Bass, a former competitive bodybuilder and fitness writer, even if he is the subject of several books—such as Lee Berquist’s Second Wind and Bill Pearl’s Legends of the Iron Game. He’s even written 10 books of his own. Unlike other fitness impresarios like Jack LaLanne, Bill Phillips, and Tony Horton, Bass eschews the spotlight and self-branding in lieu of thoughtful articles steeped in sports science and physiology. He is the thinking man’s trainer: a wise, reclusive, Yoda-like figure hidden in the high desert serving up insights that are often years ahead of their time.

An early fitness adopter, Bass helped popularize such concepts as periodization and eating nutrient-dense, calorie-light whole foods, along with Olympic lifting and Tabata intervals. He was a columnist for Muscle and Fitness magazine for 16 years and tested all kinds of new training ideas to figure out which ones worked. “Clarence distinguishes himself from the usual bodybuilding and weightlifting types because he listens more than he talks,” says Ellington Darden, Ph.D., the author of 45 training books. “To me, that was an indication of his wisdom.” Of those fitness paradigms, perhaps the most significant is recognizing the importance of high-intensity weight training. “Bass was talking about high-intensity weight training at least 10 years before CrossFit made it cool,” says Dan John, a strength coach in Salt Lake City. “Ever wonder where the term ‘ripped’ came from? That was Clarence. He took the term mainstream.”

In his 20s and 30s Bass became a world-class Olympic lifter, at his peak able to snatch 245 pounds. In his 30s he started bodybuilding, whittling himself to a preposterous 2.4 percent body fat, as measured at the former Lovelace Foundation for Medical Education and Research in Albuquerque. He’s a walking anatomy chart who won Most Muscular Man at the Past-40 Mr. U.S.A. contest. That physique has remained virtually unaltered into his 70s, as documented in his numerous portraits over the years, clad in a banana hammock, clenched in classic poses, his abs resembling a giant tortoiseshell, vasculature crackling across his arms and legs like lightning.

Bass’s astonishing vigor has attracted more than a little scientific scrutiny. Every 18 months he hustles off to the Cooper Institute—the renowned Dallas-based clinic founded by Kenneth Cooper, the author of Aerobics and the guy often credited with launching America’s running craze in the 1970s—where he undergoes a battery of health and fitness tests.

“Everyone assumes your VO2 max declines with age, that it’s unavoidable,” Bass says. “But mine has barely dropped since my 30s.” Doctors routinely screen his blood to assess his ratio of high-density lipoproteins (HDL, or good cholesterol) to low-density lipoproteins (LDL, bad cholesterol), a key factor in heart health. The lower the number, the better. “A score of 4 is considered excellent. Mine is 2,” Bass says. “Every time I get tested at some clinic or another, I defy the numbers,” he adds, pausing at a high point on the hike to savor the view of the Rio Grande Valley State Park. “My testosterone level has stayed flat. I attribute that to my diet and exercise, and pushing myself to keep my fast-twitch fibers alive and well.”

Bass is a barrel-chested 5’6”. His weight has hovered between 145 and 150 for the past 30 years. His arms are still as thick as pythons. His body fat has settled around a more reasonable 5 to 7 percent. (Elite marathoners start at around 5 percent.) When I meet him in Albuquerque, he is sporting a brownish-purple bruise on top of his hairless scalp, an injury acquired from doing pullups in his garage so aggressively that he bonked his head against the bar.

“Most people don’t train hard enough,” Bass tells me. “They train too much, but not with enough intensity. You have to look at training as breaking yourself down. If you aren’t getting sore and letting yourself recover, then you’re not getting any benefit.”

Bass believes that too many people fall into the “black hole,” a no-growth zone of medium-intensity effort that neither pushes the body far enough nor allows it to recover. The solution, he insists, is a binary approach involving complementary high- and low-intensity sessions, supported by a whole-foods diet and cycling through periodized lifting programs every few months. “You have to always be looking for ways to improve,” Bass says. “Once you decide you’re just going to maintain, you’re toast.”

I arrive in Albuquerque hoping Mr. Ripped can help me beat back my mid-40s entropy. I’d succumbed to a tide of lethargy and inertia that had turned my beach-body fantasies into muffintop reality. For reasons that aren’t entirely clear—pity?—Bass agrees to lead me through one of his workouts. Typically he trains alone, either in his home gym or on museum-worthy machines that he keeps in the cinderblock office building where he practiced law and now runs Ripped Enterprises with his wife, Carol. Bass’s workouts are not fancy, more Rocky Balboa than Ivan Drago. Still, I’m nervous. This man could break me.

We do a single-set sequence, mixing classic lifts with new ones. He tracks weights and reps methodically. Typically he uses a weight that allows him to do 8 reps, and works up to 20 before changing workouts. His latest routine uses resistance bands draped over a chinup bar to simulate the SkiErg. Made by Concept2, this machine is Bass’s most recent obsession. In the early 2000s, Bass began competing in the stationary rower 500m, reaching fourth in his weight class worldwide. Now he is trying to do the same on the SkiErg. “There’s one person ahead of me right now, a 70-year-old woman,” he says. “She’s tough.”

That bass has been able to attain his remarkable fitness through just two tough workouts a week seems incredible to me. I wondered if he might be a freak of nature, predisposed to supreme fitness. But Bass is dismissive of that idea.

“I’m the only lean one in my family!” he says. “It’s not productive to think about genes anyway, because lifestyle has a tremendous impact on the way those genes are expressed. I’ve simply optimized my genetic potential.”

Such optimization relies on something Bass calls “effort-based training.” This theory maintains that because muscle fibers fire according to intensity (first the slow-twitch fibers and then the fast-twitch ones), you have to dig deep in your workouts to access fast-twitch muscle tissue. This is especially important as you get older, since disuse of these fibers leads to their deterioration. “It’s so simple, yet so meaningful,” says Bass. “You don’t have to lift heavy, you have to lift hard.”

Peterson also points to a recent study in the journal Cell Metabolism that revealed the value of HIIT for reversing many age-related issues.

Postworkout, we sit down for a recovery meal. If abs are made in the kitchen, then Mr. Ripped’s abs are made by his wife. Every week she makes Bass’s “Old Reliable,” a mix of whole grains, vegetables, berries, nuts, and a little whole milk. She also makes him a vegetable-bean mixture. “I was pushing a whole-foods diet back in 1980, when everyone was running away from it,” he says. “The only thing I’ve really changed is adding a bit more fat to the mix, like whole milk—which tastes better anyway.”

Bass’s brief, brutish workouts would leave most hardened veterans wobbly, but it’s between workouts when the magic happens—the phase of supercompensation that translates to long-term gains. On his off days, Bass takes a brisk walk in the morning and another one after lunch, aiming for 6,000 steps total. He splurges on an occasional dessert and hardly ever drinks alcohol, except for a rare Bass Ale at a local Irish pub. His diet is a mix of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, moderate amounts of protein, and healthy fats.

“Do you like sardines?” he asks as he pulls a tin from his fridge and thunks it on the counter. “For breakfast I have Old Reliable and mix hard-boiled eggs and sardines packed in mustard. God, I love it. It’s got all the nutrients I need and tastes great. With a diet like mine, it’s almost impossible to overeat.” He avoids most processed foods, with the exception of protein-rich Tiger’s Milk Bars because “they taste good and are easy to digest.” Dinner is usually a large salad and the vegetable-bean mixture, sometimes with slices of chicken or some nuts.

This is great, all very holistic and reasonable. But what about drugs? Didn’t he use steroids as a bodybuilder?

“Yes. And I wrote all about that in Ripped 1, my first book,” he says. “It’s a very nonjudgmental take. But lifetime training is my goal, and steroids are completely counterintuitive.” He hasn’t taken any since 1980.

“You have to make it sustainable,” Bass says. “I love my life. My program is one I actually enjoy. I’m not putting myself through some hair-shirt type thing. My sex life is terrific. I have a pretty good erection. Working out helps with all this. It gets blood to your brain, your heart, and your member.”

I’d arrived feeling glum about entering my second half-century, but Mr. Ripped has reenergized me. You can’t avoid aging, but you can make it work pretty well. I think I’m living proof of that.”
