When a surgical wound appears red, is it an indication of infection or fat necrosis?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

A red surgical wound can signal either a postoperative infection or fat necrosis, and distinguishing between them requires evaluating additional signs and symptoms.

Key differences at a glance:

Infection: Redness spreads outward, wound feels warm, may drain pus or cloudy fluid, often accompanied by fever, increasing pain, or swelling.

[Red Surgical](https://neurocurrent.china-akan.com/blog/6216777437.html)

Fat necrosis: Redness is localized, a firm lump may form under the skin, usually painless, no fever, and drainage (if any) is oily or yellowish.

Because these conditions can look similar but require very different treatments, it's important not to guess. Early identification of an infection can prevent serious complications, while recognizing fat necrosis can spare you unnecessary worry and interventions.

Explanation

When a surgical wound looks red, it can mean different things. Redness is a common sign of both infection and fat necrosis. Infection usually comes with other symptoms like swelling, heat, pain, or pus. Fat necrosis, on the other hand, might not have these symptoms and is more about the fat tissue under the skin changing or dying, which can also make the area feel firm or lumpy.

Infection:

Signs: Redness, swelling, warmth, pain, and sometimes pus.

Cause: Usually from bacteria getting into the wound.

Fat Necrosis:

Signs: Redness and a firm or lumpy feeling under the skin.

Cause: Often happens when fat tissue under the skin is damaged.

Both conditions need a healthcare provider to look at them to know for sure. They may use information like how the wound looks and feels, and sometimes tests, to figure out what is going on.
